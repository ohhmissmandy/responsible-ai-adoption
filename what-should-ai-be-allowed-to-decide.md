# What Should AI Be Allowed to Decide?

Most conversations about AI right now are about capability: what a model can do, how well it does it, how fast it's improving. That's the wrong first question for most organizations. The question that actually matters is narrower and less exciting: what should this system be allowed to decide on its own, without a person checking first?

Capability and authority are not the same thing. A tool can be extremely good at something and still be a bad candidate for full autonomy, because the cost of the rare wrong answer can outweigh the benefit of the many right ones. Deciding what AI should own outright, what it should only recommend, and what it should merely help a person think through is a different exercise than evaluating how smart the system is.

 I think about it as three tiers, not because reality is ever this clean, but because it forces the right question early instead of after something's already gone wrong:

- **Assist.** The system surfaces information, options, or a first draft. A person still makes the call and is expected to actually engage with it, not rubber-stamp it.
- **Recommend.** The system proposes a specific action, and a person approves or rejects it before anything happens.
- **Decide.** The system can execute the action on its own, without case-by-case human approval, though that doesn't mean no one is watching. Monitoring, audits, exception handling, or after-the-fact review can still be part of how it runs.

Organizations often feel real pressure to push decisions toward "decide," because more automation usually means more speed, more scale, or lower cost, and those gains are immediate while the failure case can feel abstract until it isn't. The sorting question that matters most is what happens if this is wrong. Not "how often is it wrong," which is a capability question, but what the actual consequence is the one time it is. A recommendation engine suggesting a document to read can fail constantly and cost almost nothing. A system deciding whether to flag, block, or approve something with real consequences for a person or the business is a different category entirely, even if its accuracy is excellent.

Reversibility is the other lens I keep coming back to. A decision that's easy to catch and undo can tolerate more autonomy, because a mistake is a hiccup, not a crisis. A decision that's hard or impossible to unwind deserves a person in the loop even when the system is usually right, because "usually" isn't the same as "always," and the cost of the exception has to be survivable.

Underneath both of those is accountability, and this is the part people skip past fastest. If a decision goes wrong, someone has to be able to explain what happened and be answerable for it. That doesn't have to mean a person did the task manually. It can mean a person owns the outcome and has real ability to review the decision, challenge it, intervene before or after the fact, and escalate it when something doesn't look right. But if no one can answer "who's accountable here" in a sentence, and point to what they can actually do about it, the decision isn't ready to be handed over, regardless of how good the underlying model is.

None of this is actually new territory. Anyone who has run technology change in an environment with real operational risk already knows this discipline: testing before a full rollout, piloting before scaling so failure shows up early and small, monitoring once something's live, and keeping a rollback plan for anything that touches real outcomes. AI decision rights build on that same foundation, though AI adds its own governance questions on top of it, like how a model's behavior can shift over time or how to explain a decision that wasn't explicitly programmed step by step. Either way, the instinct is the same: figure out the blast radius before you find out about it the hard way, not after.

The practical version of all this is a habit, not a full framework: before asking whether AI can do something, ask what happens if it's wrong, whether that's something you can undo, and who's on the hook if it isn't. Those three questions are a strong starting filter, not a full clearance. Privacy, security, regulatory requirements, fairness, and data quality all carry their own use-case-specific controls, and any of them can rule out autonomy even when consequence, reversibility, and accountability look fine on their own. If any of it gives you pause, the system should be assisting or recommending, not deciding, until that changes.
